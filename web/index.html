1. Introduction

Short-horizon load forecasting is a canonical CS problem: server load, network throughput, click-through rate, ICU vitals — they all share the same mathematical shape. We study it through a deceptively simple instance: predicting the next 4 hours of household appliance power consumption (Wh) from a 16-hour multivariate sensor history.

The puzzle. Our milestone report uncovered an anomaly: a 675K-parameter MLP underperformed a zero-parameter Persistence baseline on MAE. Instinct says "training bug"; the correct answer (after reading the dataset's original paper) is that strong naive baselines are a property of this dataset, not a symptom of broken training — the dataset paper reports test R² = 0.57 with GBM despite training R² = 0.97, so most of the target's variance is irreducible human-driven noise.

Contributions

  1. First systematic multi-horizon benchmark on the Candanedo 2017 dataset, covering 6 model families × 5 horizons × 11 experimental axes, fully reproducible.
  2. DPMixer, a deliberately minimal four-branch additive mixer composed of well-known components.
  3. TC-DPMixer (time-conditioned gating) — our main architectural contribution. Branch gates become input-dependent via a tiny MLP over the forecast-step's time features (extending MoLE [Ni et al., AISTATS 2024] to heterogeneous additive branches). The learned gates form an interpretable 24-h schedule.
  4. 30 % MAE reduction from milestone (49.3 → 34.83 Wh) using a 68K-parameter model — within 0.6 Wh of the 412K-parameter iTransformer while being 6× smaller.

2. Dataset and Background

UCI Appliances Energy Prediction dataset [link]: 19,735 records at 10-min cadence over 4.5 months (Jan–May 2016), collected in a passive house in Stambruges, Belgium via ZigBee sensors plus airport weather data.

Why baselines are hard to beat

At 10-min cadence appliance use is strongly autocorrelated — most of the time it's flat, with brief human-driven spikes (kettle, oven, hairdryer) that account for most of the variance but are inherently unpredictable. The original GBM reports train R² = 0.97 vs test R² = 0.57, indicating most test variance is irreducible. Our sanity check confirms this: test variance / train variance = 0.73 and test max (850 Wh) is below train max (1080 Wh).

3. Related Work

  • DLinear (Zeng et al., AAAI 2023): single linear layer per trend/seasonal branch beats most Transformer LTSF models.
  • TSMixer (Chen et al., TMLR 2023): alternating time-mixing and channel-mixing MLPs.
  • TimeMixer (Wang et al., ICLR 2024): multi-scale decomposable mixing — closest competing architecture to DPMixer.
  • N-BEATS / N-HiTS: stacked residual blocks summing to the final prediction (additive philosophy).
  • MoLE (Ni et al., AISTATS 2024): timestamp-conditioned MLP router over linear experts. TC-DPMixer extends this with (a) heterogeneous inductive-bias branches, (b) forecast-step conditioning, and (c) a 24-h schedule visualization.
  • Sequence-to-Point NILM (Zhang et al., AAAI 2018): classify + magnitude heads for appliance disaggregation; we borrow this pattern as auxiliary forecasting heads in SS-DPMixer.

DPMixer architecture

Given input x ∈ RL×C:

B1 = x[-1, 0:1] · 1H                          (persistence anchor)
B2 = DLinear(x[:, 0])                          (target decomposition)
B3 = MixerHead(MLPMixer(x[-1, :]))             (channel mix at last step)
B4 = MixerHead(MLPMixer(x[:, 0]))              (time mix on target)
ŷ  = α1B1 + α2B2 + α3B3 + α4B4                  (learned gates)

In TC-DPMixer the four scalar gates αi are replaced by a small MLP that outputs per-sample gate values conditioned on the input's last-timestep time features.

Training recipe

Adam (lr=1e-3, wd=1e-4); ReduceLROnPlateau (patience 5); early-stopping (patience 10); seed reset to 42 before every fit; grad-clip = 1.0; L1 loss for the headline configuration.

5.3 Feature engineering (Workstream A)

Surprising finding: Using only the target's own history beats using all 26 sensors. Adding time encodings + log target cuts DLinear's test MAE from 49.3 to 37.9 Wh (−23 %).
FIG 2
Test MAE for 10 feature configurations. Indoor T/RH react to energy use after it occurs (radiative heat from stove, oven, etc.) so they have low predictive value.

5.4 Training-loss ablation (Workstream B)

Switching MSE → L1 reduces MAE by 5–20 Wh for every model. The milestone's "MLP < Persistence on MAE" puzzle resolves into a metric-objective mismatch: MSE training favors mean-predictions, which look conservative on a long-tailed target.

5.6 Modern Transformer variants (Workstream D)

iTransformer-base (412K params): test MAE 44.4 Wh with persistence anchoring, vs. PatchTST-base (142K params) at 51.3 Wh — cross-variate attention substantially outperforms cross-time attention on this multivariate dataset. Larger variants (iTrans-large 2.4M params) do not improve MAE, supporting "structure beats scale."

7. Discussion and Takeaways

  1. Inductive bias > model size. A 4.7K-parameter linear model beats a 678K Transformer on this dataset; a 68K Mixer with learned branch gates matches a 412K Transformer. When data is moderate and noisy, sensible structure matters more than scale.
  2. Training objective dictates apparent quality. MSE training makes models look bad on MAE evaluation; switching to L1 loss (one line of code) flips the apparent ranking. Always train against an objective close to your evaluation metric.
  3. A strong baseline is a discovery, not a bug. When the naive baseline wins, the right response is to ask why the data permits that, not to discard the experiment.

8. Limitations

Single dataset; cross-house generalization untested. Hyperparameters not exhaustively searched per workstream. SSL uses only masked-patch MSE (contrastive variants like TS2Vec not compared). DPMixer's components are each derivative of prior work (NLinear, DLinear, TSMixer); architectural novelty is limited to per-branch time-conditioned gating (MoLE-style) and the empirical benchmark itself.
