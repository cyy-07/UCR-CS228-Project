\begin{abstract}
Accurate multi-horizon forecasting of household energy consumption requires combining multiple inductive biases, yet most existing models apply a single uniform forecasting mechanism across all time scales. We propose TC-DPMixer, a lightweight forecasting model that combines heterogeneous predictive components through a time-encoding routing mechanism, producing an interpretable time-of-day dependent policy. On the weekly forecasting task, TC-DPMixer achieves $214.44 \pm 0.75$ Wh/h, outperforming iTransformer at $219.91 \pm 2.42$ while using significantly fewer parameters (187K vs. 440K), with a statistically significant paired improvement.  The model consists of four standard forecasting components: a persistence anchor, a decomposition branch, a channel-mixing module, and a time-mixing module  with interpretability concentrated entirely in the time-encoding gate.On the short-horizon task, performance is slightly worse than iTransformer (34.99 vs. 34.15 Wh), indicating a trade-off between efficiency and short-term accuracy.
This is a single-household study and cross-domain generalization is not evaluated. Code: \href{https://github.com/cyy-07/UCR-CS228-Project}{github.com/cyy-07/UCR-CS228-Project}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time-series forecasting is significant in many tasks, such as modeling human behavior and anomaly detection. In recent years, we have witnessed so many models that are powerful and have achieved strong prediction accuracy on different benchmarks. However, forecasting tasks can vary substantially in their temporal scope, and different horizons may require different predictive mechanisms. For example, redicting the next few hours and predicting the next week are often treated as the same forecasting problem. Short-horizon forecasts often rely heavily on recent observations, while longer horizons may depend more on recurring seasonal and temporal patterns.

To explore energy time-series forecasting, we study the UCI Appliances Energy dataset \citep{candanedo2017}, a 4.5-month single-household energy record sampled every 10 minutes. We consider two forecasting settings: a short-horizon task that predicts the next 4 hours from the previous 16 hours, and a long-horizon task that predicts the next week from the previous week.

In recent years, Transformer-based models such as iTransformer \citep{itransformer} have achieved strong results on many forecasting benchmarks, while some light-weight model like DLinear \citep{dlinear} showed that a much simpler linear architecture can remain highly competitive in some settings. Mixer-style models such as TSMixer \citep{tsmixer} have provided another lightweight alternative by modeling interactions across time and channels through MLP-based components. Meanwhile, routing-based approaches such as Mixture-of-Linear-Experts (MoLE) \citep{mole} suggest that different forecasting mechanisms can be combined dynamically rather than relying on a single predictive pathway. These results suggest that different forecasting mechanisms may be useful for different parts of the prediction problem, motivating the use of input-dependent routing strategies to combine them within a single framework. This motivates the following question:

Can a lightweight forecasting model combine multiple predictive mechanisms while remaining easy to inspect?

To this end, we propose TC-DPMixer, a four-branch additive forecasting model consisting of a persistence anchor, a decomposition branch, a channel-mixing branch, and a time-mixing branch. A small time-encoding gate determines the relative contribution of each branch throughout the day. Because the gate depends only on time-encoding features, the resulting routing policy can be visualized directly as a 24-hour schedule.


\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We propose TC-DPMixer, a four-branch additive forecasting model whose routing policy is determined by time-encoding features alone, allowing the learned 24-hour schedule to be visualized directly.

\item We establish a reproducible benchmark on the UCI Appliances Energy dataset across short- and long-horizon forecasting tasks, including statistical significance tests, block cross-validation, and efficiency analysis.

\item We show that the learned routing policy remains consistent across gate visualization, branch ablation, and a reduced model variant, providing a coherent picture of how the forecasting mechanisms are used on this dataset.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Four-branch backbone (DPMixer).}
A window $x_{1:L}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times C}$ holds $L$ steps of $C$
input channels --- appliance load, lights, room temperatures and
humidities, six outdoor weather variables, and six time-encoding
features. The forecast target
$\hat{y}_{L{+}1:L{+}H}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}$ is the appliance channel
and $H\in\{24, 168\}$ controls the short and weekly horizons.
DPMixer is a weighted sum of four parallel branches:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{t+h}
\;=\; \alpha_{0}\,x_{t,0}
\;+\; \alpha_{1}\,\mathbf{B}_{\text{dec}}
\;+\; \alpha_{2}\,\mathbf{B}_{\text{ch}}
\;+\; \alpha_{3}\,\mathbf{B}_{\text{tm}}.
\label{eq:dpmixer}
\end{equation}
The persistence anchor repeats the last observed target value
$x_{t,0}$ across the forecast horizon. The three learned branches are
$\mathbf{B}_{\text{dec}}$, a DLinear-style trend $+$ seasonal
decomposition on the target channel \citep{dlinear} (moving-average
kernel 25, two parallel linear heads);
$\mathbf{B}_{\text{ch}}$, two MLP-Mixer blocks acting on the last
timestep across all $C$ channels followed by a linear projection to
$H$; and $\mathbf{B}_{\text{tm}}$, two MLP-Mixer blocks acting along
the time axis on the target channel followed by a linear projection
to $H$ \citep{tsmixer}. Hidden dimensions and dropout are listed in
Appendix~\ref{si:impl}.

\paragraph{Time-encoding gate.}
In TC-DPMixer the four branch gates are produced on-the-fly by a
small MLP that reads only the time-encoding features at the last
input step, $c_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{k}(c_{t})
\;=\; 2\,\sigma\!\big(\mathrm{MLP}_{k}(c_{t})\big)
\;\in\;[0, 2],
\qquad k\in\{0, 1, 2, 3\},
\label{eq:tc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MLP}$ is a $6\to 16\to 4$ feed-forward network with
ReLU activation functions and the output layer initialized to zero, so the
network starts with all four branch weights equal to one. Unlike softmax-based routing, the four gates are not normalized across branches. Each gate independently scales its corresponding branch within $[0,2]$. The routing idea
follows Mixture-of-Linear-Experts \citep{mole}; the experts here are
heterogeneous inductive-bias branches rather than copies of the same
linear head used in the original MoLE paper. Also, because $c_{t}$ contains
no weather or load history, the 24-hour schedule
$\alpha_{k}(\cdot)$ is a fixed function of clock time
(Figure~\ref{fig:gate}).

  \centering

  \caption{\textbf{TC-DPMixer architecture.} Four parallel branches
    (persistence anchor, target decomposition, channel mixer, time
    mixer) are summed under per-branch gates. A small MLP reads only
    the time-encoding feature vector $c_{t}$ and outputs four gates
    $\alpha_{k} = 2\,\sigma(\mathrm{MLP}_{k}(c_{t})) \in [0, 2]$; the

\end{figure}

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We use the UCI Appliances Energy dataset \citep{candanedo2017}
resampled to 1-hour buckets and split chronologically 70/15/15.
Baselines are Persistence, Seasonal-Naive, DLinear, PatchTST,
iTransformer, and the un-gated DPMixer. All learned models are
trained with Adam ($\eta{=}10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), $L_{1}$
loss on $\log(1+y)$ targets, batch size 64, early stopping with
patience 10, and five seeds per configuration; hyperparameters were
tuned on the validation split only. We report mean$\pm$std over five
seeds. Short-horizon error is
in Wh (MAE over the next 4\,h) and weekly error is in Wh/h (MAE
averaged over 168\,h). All learned models are built on the
persistence residual $\hat{y}=x_{t,0}+f(X)$, which is needed because
Persistence itself has a negative $R^{2}$ on the short split
(Appendix~\ref{si:tables}).

\paragraph{Main results.}
TC-DPMixer achieves the best weekly MAE at
$214.44 \pm 0.75$\,Wh/h; iTransformer leads on the short horizon at
$34.15 \pm 0.48$\,Wh, with TC-DPMixer in second place
(Table~\ref{tab:headline}). The weekly paired difference is
5.47\,Wh/h, with 95\,\% bootstrap CI $[-6.93, -4.11]$ and paired
Cohen's $d=-2.87$. On the short horizon, TC-DPMixer trails
iTransformer by only 0.84\,Wh while using approximately one-sixth of
the parameters (67K vs.\ 412K), suggesting that similar predictive
performance can be achieved with a much smaller model. The
time-encoding gate also improves the weekly MAE from 217.09\,Wh/h
to 214.44\,Wh/h, indicating that routing based on temporal context
contributes beyond the un-gated DPMixer backbone.

\paragraph{Gate analysis.}
The time-encoding gate is directly readable as a 24-hour policy
(Figure~\ref{fig:gate}): the seasonal weight dominates overnight,
while the trend weight rises as activity picks up. For example,
around 2--3\,a.m., when household demand follows a regular nightly
baseline, the gate places almost all the weight on the seasonal
branch. Around 5--7\,p.m., when usage becomes activity-dependent
(cooking, returning home, evening appliances) and energy
consumption becomes unstable, the gate shifts toward the trend
branch, which is responsive to the most recent observations rather
than the regular seasonal pattern. To verify that the gate encodes
only time features, we perturb the outdoor temperature by
$\pm 10\,^{\circ}$C and observe zero RMS change in the gate output,
since temperature is not part of its input.

\paragraph{Robustness.}
Sliding the chronological cut from 55\,\% to 70\,\% train ratio
gives four block-CV folds (Figure~\ref{fig:blockcv}; full per-fold
numbers in Table~\ref{tab:blockcv}). TC-DPMixer attains the lowest
weekly MAE on every fold, with per-fold gaps of 10.9, 6.3, 13.3, and
5.9\,Wh/h over iTransformer; on the short task the three models
cluster within 1\,Wh and iTransformer wins each fold by 0.85\,Wh on
average, at six times the parameter count of TC-DPMixer.
Permutation importance is dominated by self-history and hour-of-day:
permuting the appliance channel raises test MAE by 7.35\,Wh and
\texttt{hour\_cos} by 3.72\,Wh, while outdoor temperature contributes
0.06\,Wh. These results indicate that temporal information is substantially more
important than weather variables on this dataset. A stripped variant retaining only the persistence anchor, the
time-mixer branch, and the time-encoding gate remains competitive
(Appendix~\ref{si:minimal}), suggesting that a substantial fraction
of the weekly forecasting signal can be captured by the routing
mechanism and the temporal branch alone.

\paragraph{From regression to spike detection.}
Throughout this report we have measured models by exact Wh MAE. In
practice, a utility operator often does not need the precise load:
what really matters is whether the next operating window will
contain a demand \emph{spike} that strains the grid. We therefore
re-frame the task as a binary classification problem --- for each
future time step, predict whether the appliance load exceeds a
training-set percentile threshold --- and replace TC-DPMixer's
regression head with a class-balanced BCE head while keeping the
four-branch backbone unchanged. The model then outputs a sequence
of logits, one per future step, indicating whether that step is
likely to exceed the threshold. Under the two threshold choices we
tested (top 10\,\%, $p_{90}$, and top 5\,\%, $p_{95}$, of the
training distribution), the backbone attains macro AUC-ROC of 0.81
and 0.79 respectively --- the highest among all contenders we ran,
including a flat logistic-regression baseline at 0.79 / 0.77 (full
per-horizon table in Appendix~\ref{si:spike}). On an entirely
held-out week the 168-hour forecast already tracks both the diurnal
baseline and the individual spikes hour-by-hour
(Figure~\ref{fig:spike}). The inductive biases built into the
backbone for regression therefore carry over to the operationally
more useful task of detecting when a spike is coming.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

TC-DPMixer was motivated by a simple question: can multiple
forecasting mechanisms be combined through a routing policy that
still remains easy to inspect? On the UCI Appliances Energy dataset,
the answer appears to be yes. The model achieves the lowest weekly
forecasting error among the methods we tested, while the
time-encoding gate produces a directly readable 24-hour
schedule that can be visualized without examining internal
activations or attention maps.


At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. The
study uses a single-household dataset covering approximately four and
a half months, so the learned routing schedule may reflect properties
of this particular home rather than general household behavior.
Moreover, the dataset contains more than ten thousand observations,
yet the current experiments only evaluate fixed-window forecasting
tasks. Future work could investigate more challenging settings such
as month-ahead forecasting, cross-season prediction, and transfer
across different households.

In conclusion, three observations emerge from this study.

First,
the usefulness of a forecasting mechanism depends strongly on the
prediction horizon: time-encoding routing contributes most on
the weekly task, while short-horizon forecasting remains largely
driven by recent observations. 


Second, temporal and time-encoding information appear to be more
informative than most weather variables on this dataset, suggesting
that household energy consumption is primarily governed by recurring
behavioral patterns. 

Third, the same TC-DPMixer backbone can support both energy forecasting and spike detection with only a change in the output head, suggesting that the forecasting mechanisms learned by the model remain useful beyond a single prediction objective.

\section{Related work}
\label{si:related}

\paragraph{Linear and decomposition baselines.}
DLinear and NLinear \citep{dlinear} showed that a single linear layer
on a trend--seasonal decomposition matches or beats deeper Transformer
forecasters on many long-horizon benchmarks. N-BEATS \citep{nbeats}
reaches similar conclusions with stacks of fully connected residual
blocks. These models are cheap and easy to audit but treat all
horizons identically; on weekly-scale household data the relative
importance of trend, season, and exogenous channels varies across
the day.

\paragraph{Mixer architectures.}
TSMixer \citep{tsmixer} and TimeMixer \citep{timemixer} introduce
time-mixing and channel-mixing MLPs as a transformer alternative.
PatchTST \citep{patchtst} and iTransformer \citep{itransformer} keep
self-attention but transpose its axis to model channels as tokens.
These models improve accuracy but spend parameter count and inference
cost to do so, and their attention maps over channels do not give the
operator a 24-hour schedule to sanity-check.

\paragraph{Routed mixtures.}
Mixture-of-Linear-Experts (MoLE) \citep{mole} routes linear
forecasters through a softmax gate. We adopt the routing form but
apply it to heterogeneous inductive-bias branches rather than
homogeneous linear experts, and we condition the gate on
time-encoding features only so its policy is a fixed 24-hour curve. NILM
sequence-to-point work \citep{nilm} motivates our use of the
appliance channel as the primary target.


\section{Implementation details}
\label{si:impl}

\paragraph{Branches.}
The target-decomposition branch uses a DLinear-style 25-point moving-
average kernel; trend and seasonal projections are both
\texttt{Linear(seq\_len $\to$ pred\_len)}. The channel mixer reads the
last timestep across all $C$ feature channels, passes it through two
MLP-Mixer blocks (hidden dim $128$, dropout $0.1$), and projects to
pred\_len. The time mixer applies the same MLP-Mixer pair along the
$L$ axis on the target channel only (hidden dim $128$, dropout $0.1$),
then projects to pred\_len.

\paragraph{Time-encoding gate.}
The gate MLP takes the 6-dim time-encoding feature vector
(hour\_sin/cos, weekday\_sin/cos, weekend\_flag, NSM) at the
last input step and outputs four branch weights via
$\sigma(\mathrm{MLP}(c_t)) \cdot 2$, giving weights in
$[0, 2]^{4}$. The MLP is
$\mathrm{Linear}(6 \to 16) \to \mathrm{ReLU} \to \mathrm{Linear}(16 \to 4)$
with the output layer initialised to zero so that the initial
branch weights equal one.

\paragraph{Training.}
All models train with Adam ($\eta=10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$),
$L_1$ loss on a $\log(1+y)$-transformed target, batch size 64, and
early stopping with patience 10. We use chronological 70/15/15 splits
on the headline runs and 55/15/30, 60/15/25, 65/15/20, 70/15/15 splits
on the block-CV runs. Five seeds (\texttt{42--46}) are used for every
mean$\pm$std reported in the main paper. No hyperparameters were
tuned on the test split.


\paragraph{Hardware.}
All experiments were conducted on the UCR BCOE High-Performance
Computing Cluster using a single NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU under
PyTorch 2.4.0+cu121 in eager mode. The latency benchmark in
Table~\ref{tab:lat} used 100 warm-up forwards followed by
1{,}000 timed forwards per configuration.


\section{Design-space sensitivity around the four-branch backbone}
\label{si:minimal}

The four-branch TC-DPMixer reported in the main paper is the
headline configuration: the persistence anchor, target decomposition,
channel mixer, and time mixer together provide coverage over
heterogeneous inductive biases, which is what the time-encoding gate
then routes between. To probe how sensitive the headline numbers are to
the choice of these branches, we report two complementary checks ---
a per-module contribution analysis and a stripped configuration.

\paragraph{Per-module contribution.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the short-task test MAE for the full
four-branch DPMixer and for variants in which one branch is removed
at a time. The differences are within a fraction of one Wh of the
full model; the time-mixer branch is the only component whose removal
appreciably degrades MAE, and an only-persistence configuration is
substantially worse. The result suggests that part of the
inductive-bias coverage provided by the four-branch design may be
redundant on this dataset.

\paragraph{Stripped configuration.}
A stripped configuration that retains only the persistence anchor,

headline configuration because its inductive-bias coverage is what
the time-encoding gate is designed to route between; the stripped
configuration is reported as a sensitivity check.

\section{Spike-detection details}
\label{si:spike}

We re-frame the regression input as a binary classification problem:
for each of the next $H$ steps, predict a $\{0, 1\}$ label
indicating whether the future load at that step exceeds a
training-set percentile threshold (top 10\,\%, $p_{90}$, or top
5\,\%, $p_{95}$). The TC-DPMixer backbone is kept unchanged and its
regression head is replaced with a class-balanced BCE classification
head. We compare against three baselines: \emph{chance} (predicts
the prior positive rate), \emph{last-value} (uses the last observed
target value thresholded at $\tau$), and \emph{logreg-flat}
(\texttt{Linear($L \cdot C \to H$)} trained with BCE).
Table~\ref{tab:spike} reports macro-averaged metrics over the
forecast horizon; numbers come from
\texttt{results/spike\_detect.csv}.

\section{Additional ablations and early experiments}

the milestone stage of the project. They explain how we arrived at the
headline recipe (\texttt{no\_rv + time + log\_target}, $L_1$ loss) and

informative features --- whose findings did not make it into the main
paper but motivated the inductive-bias choices in the DPMixer
backbone.

\paragraph{Weakly-informative features (rv1, rv2).}
The Candanedo 2017 dataset ships with two columns, \texttt{rv1} and
\texttt{rv2}, that the dataset authors injected as uniformly random
variables for a feature-importance sanity check. Including them as
input features lowers short-horizon MAE for three of the four deep
models by 2--4\,Wh and slightly raises it for DLinear
(Table~\ref{tab:rv}). On this dataset the two random columns appear to
act as a weak input-perturbation regulariser for the over-parameterised
models, while the already strongly regularised DLinear (4.7K
parameters) does not benefit. We exclude these columns from the
headline recipe to keep the input set free of features known to be
uninformative by construction.
